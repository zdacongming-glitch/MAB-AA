                                                     Abstract
              We study reliable maintenance of all relatively near-optimal arms while collecting rewards
          when new arms arrive exogenously and remain available. Reward laws are stationary, but arrivals
          change the membership boundary. Reporting progress is measured by cumulative unresolved
          arm time outside a prescribed boundary band, with simultaneous correctness of all labels. One
          uncertain arrival can obstruct many earlier certifications. A common-reference lower bound
          quantifies this effect, and matching worst-schedule area bounds yield an arrival-growth boundary
          for joint sublinear performance. On specified repeated-update families, temporary membership
          tasks impose rapid-maintenance regret costs, while separate constructions attain deferred and
          intermediate service levels. We develop Flexible Certificate-Cost Planning (F-CCP) as a tunable
          implementation. It combines sequential evidence and local certificate planning with a chosen
          reward kernel and separate observable budgets for reward and certification. Its guarantees include
          label reliability, unconditional expected-regret protection, and service-rate area bounds that
          retain budget rejections. Fixed-checkpoint and activation-budgeted constructions remain distinct
          analytical witnesses. Frozen paired experiments show lower mean costs in many finite-budget
          comparisons, together with exceptions, heavier regret tails, and nonzero reporting failures. These
          results do not characterize a general regret–maintenance frontier.

Keywords: sequential experimentation; arriving arms; near-optimal set maintenance; dynamic regret;
confidence sequences.


1        Introduction
Sequential experiments often acquire new candidates while earlier ones remain available. An
allocation rule must learn which candidates yield high rewards. An experimenter may also need to
know which of all available candidates are within a specified tolerance of the current best. These

objectives need not favor the same observations. A candidate can be sufficiently inferior to receive
little reward-driven exploration and still be difficult to classify relative to the near-optimality
boundary.
     We study these objectives together under permanent exogenous arrivals and stationary reward
laws. If r∗ (t) is the largest mean among the arms available at time t, an arm is relatively near-optimal
when its mean is at least r∗ (t) − ε. Reports certify membership, certify exclusion, or leave the arm
unresolved. We require one simultaneous correctness event for all labels at all times. To rule out
indefinite abstention, we charge the cumulative unresolved time of arms whose means lie more than
η from the membership boundary. The question is how much regret is necessary, and how much is
attainable, under this reporting requirement.
     New arrivals affect the task in two ways. An uncertain new candidate may be better than
the incumbent by enough to invalidate many existing positive reports. Evidence about that one
candidate is then relevant to many reports at once. A later improvement can also make an earlier
difficult membership question unnecessary: a candidate close to the old boundary may be far below
the new one. Its existing upper confidence bound can be combined with a new reference’s lower
bound to certify exclusion. Neither effect requires the reward law of an old arm to change.
     Our observation model and regret comparator have established precedents. Permanent arrivals
form a monotone special case of sleeping-bandit availability (Kleinberg et al., 2010); ballooning
bandits already compare reward with the best arm currently available (Ghalme et al., 2021).
Exogenous arrivals differ from drawing arms on demand from a reservoir (Berry et al., 1997) and
from the disappearing actions of mortal bandits (Chakrabarti et al., 2008). Benchmark improvements
here arise from new arms, whereas nonstationary bandits allow the reward distributions of existing
arms to change (Besbes et al., 2014; Auer et al., 2019). Sequential allocation and adaptive
experimentation have a long statistical history (Robbins, 1952; Lai and Robbins, 1985; Lai, 1987);
the expanding set of actions retains that allocation problem and adds an evolving inferential target.
     For a fixed arm set, identifying every relatively near-optimal arm is the ALL-ε problem (Mason
et al., 2020). Joint reward and identification objectives also have established precedents (Degenne
et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2026; Cho et al., 2025). Our task requires continuing reports under
arrivals, with all-time label reliability and an explicit cumulative cost for unresolved arm time. The
distinction concerns the reporting protocol and its cost, rather than a new arriving-arm model. The
simultaneous recovery scales used in our lower bounds build on existing information inequalities
and multiple-recovery arguments (Kaufmann et al., 2016; Malloy and Nowak, 2014; Jamieson and
Jain, 2018).
     We give three results about maintenance cost. First, a common-reference lower bound shows how
one uncertain arrival can obstruct many other certifications. Its information queue accumulates cost
over time within one reward environment. Second, we obtain matching worst-schedule orders for
cumulative unresolved area, including finite-horizon truncation, and derive the polynomial arrival
boundary for jointly sublinear area and expected regret. This worst-case lower bound already
occurs in an initial static batch; the common-reference result describes a separate consequence of
continuing arrivals. Third, on a specified repeated-update family, rapid resolution of temporary

budget admits a logarithmic regret upper bound. A nonvanishing-probability lower bound holds in
one fixed environment under the same cumulative-area requirement. The deferred endpoint has
only an upper bound. A separate activation-budgeted witness covers an intermediate interval on a
more strongly separated family; the general causal frontier under overlapping arrivals remains open.
    Fixed checkpoints make attainable costs explicit, but their quotas can consume a moderate
budget before useful reports appear. F-CCP uses every observation to update valid reporting
evidence and plans one certificate observation at a time. A predictable priority controls the proposed
service rate. Separate budgets limit the virtual-index cost of reward choices and of certification, so
exploration by the reward kernel does not exhaust the certification account. The resulting guarantees
concern reliability, regret protection, and conditional service bounds; the local planning proxy is not
a solution of the full statistical allocation problem. The fixed-checkpoint witnesses are retained for
their original guarantees in the supplement.
    The components have precedents. Betting processes provide time-uniform inference for bounded
means (Waudby-Smith and Ramdas, 2024); our particular mixture and its bounded-reward validity
are proved explicitly. Thompson sampling supplies a flexible reward rule (Thompson, 1933).
MOTS obtains fixed-arm minimax guarantees by modifying sampled scores (Jin et al., 2021). Our
protection instead constrains the virtual-index cost of the actual selected action under arrivals,
and does not transfer MOTS’s theorem to a cold-temperature Beta rule. Conservative bandits
control performance against a fixed baseline (Wu et al., 2016); the two accounts here control index
deficits and are not realized shortfalls from a separately run baseline. The spectral accounting and
MC-MOSS concentration tools are inputs from the companion work (Temporal Geometry), not a
new characterization claimed here.
    Information about future task expiration also matters. A schedule-specific comparator can defer
a temporary question in a way that one causal policy facing an unknown schedule cannot reproduce
uniformly. We retain that information distinction and an activation-budgeted intermediate-regime
result alongside the general maintenance bounds. The latter uses its own window conditions and is
not an optimality claim for F-CCP.
    Section 2 fixes the joint task and separates task parameters from allocation preferences. Section 3
presents F-CCP and its guarantees. Sections 4 and 5 give the general costs, temporary-task bounds,
and information limits. Section 6 evaluates frozen configurations, including mean improvements,
tail costs, failed reports, and the corrected same-controller comparison.

2.3    How arrivals change the reporting problem
For a fixed old arm, ∆i (t) is nondecreasing, so its true membership can change only from member
to nonmember. Its boundary distance |∆i (t) − ε| need not be monotone. A new candidate can first
create uncertainty about the current best mean, obstructing positive certification of many old arms
even when those arms remain truly near-optimal. A sufficiently good new reference can later move
the boundary past earlier candidates, making their fine classification unnecessary. Sections 4 and 5
quantify these two effects under the same reporting protocol.
    Arms with close means need not be close to the relative boundary r∗ (t) − ε. Conversely, a
candidate near that boundary can require fine evidence even when its reward gap is appreciable.

The algorithm’s observable work set is therefore defined from confidence intervals, not from the
evaluator’s true gaps or unresolved area.


3     Flexible certificate-cost planning
The reporting task need not receive a fixed fraction of every sampling budget. Evidence obtained
while collecting rewards can already certify some arms, and a small amount of additional work
can complete other reports. Flexible Certificate-Cost Planning (F-CCP) uses one set of lifetime
statistics for both purposes. Its service preference controls how often certification is attempted; two
separate budgets protect the reward allocation and reserve capacity for certification. The resulting
guarantees concern reliable labels, regret order, and service completion. They do not identify the
optimal allocation for every joint regret–area constraint.

3.5    Reliability, reward protection, and service completion
Proposition 3.1 (Reliable reports). Under the bounded stationary model, every legal predictable
choice of preferences, prices, and service actions in F-CCP satisfies P(Elab ) ≥ 1 − δ. On a common
event E of this probability, all confidence intervals cover their means at all observed counts, all
reports are correct, and every arm in Zt lies in the boundary band.
   The evidence argument does not require the proxy plan to predict its actual sampling cost. The
mixture and checkpoint errors each receive δ/2, and a union bound gives E.
Proposition 3.2 (Compatibility endpoint). Fix a legal reward kernel B. If service is off from the
start (λt ≡ 0), βR = ∞, and the complete kernel state, initialization, and random-number source
agree with those of B, the two procedures have identical actions, lifetime counts, and regret on every
potential reward realization and arrival schedule.
    This is a same-backend pathwise statement. A common seed in different random-number
backends does not provide the required coupling. The colder, guarded F-R preset is distinct from
this endpoint. Switching off service after using it does not recreate a baseline’s independent past.

6.1   Protocol and comparisons
We study reward performance and the cost of reliable reporting separately. The frozen main suite
contains 12 scenarios, 24 configurations and 96 paired replications per scenario and configuration,
giving 27,648 records. A subsequent correction of one ALL comparator contributes 1,152 additional
records. The original and corrected comparator records are both retained. The primary analysis
replaces the original adaptive ALL configuration by its corrected version, leaving 24 configurations
and 27,648 records.

                                                18
    The scenarios have 2–20 arms and horizons of 12,000–16,000. Eleven use Bernoulli rewards; one
uses bounded two-point rewards. The reporting parameters ε and η vary by scenario, while δ = .05

replication, all methods share the same potential reward stream for each arm, and the mean labels

7                     Discussion
Reliable maintenance has costs that reward allocation alone does not measure. An uncertain arrival
can obstruct many positive reports, while a later improvement can end a fine membership task.
The general lower bounds quantify these effects. The worst-area lower bound answers a different
question and is already attained by an initial static batch; the fixed-schedule and terminal-count
growth consequences retain different quantifiers.
   F-CCP adds an implementable choice of emphasis to this task. Its evidence layer uses every
observation, its certificate planner proposes a single next observation, and separate accounts control
reward and certification costs. Finite guards protect the spectral regret order but can reject

ALL comparison is distinct from the retained superseded controller result. These findings concern
the frozen suite, not uniform dominance or a general frontier.
    The activation-budgeted witness characterizes an intermediate interval on a sufficiently separated
family, with an additive reward term and its own information requirements. General causal frontiers
under unknown and overlapping arrivals remain open. The schedule-information separation does not
make every causal comparison impossible. Likewise, the one-step zero-remainder counterexample
leaves room for comparisons with an explicit adaptation cost or a restricted performance family.
    Automatic precision, reward kernels with better tail behavior, a faithful adaptation of the
original ALL-ε algorithms, and large-pool planning costs need further study. The supplement’s
eventual exact-set consistency theorem belongs to a separate revisable multiresolution procedure
under finite final arrivals and strict boundary separation. F-CCP’s coverage-event completion
outside a boundary band is a different statement.

S4     Guarantees for Flexible CCP
The reward and service results have different hypotheses. Reliability allows any legal predictable
preference, including a preference tending to zero. The regret bound uses fixed budget coefficients;
an area rate requires a positive lower bound on service while work remains. These conditions are
not supplied by the word “adaptive.”

S4.1    Compatibility and state preservation
To prove Proposition 3.2, couple the potential reward streams, arrivals, full initial kernel state, and
backend random-number source. At the first round the service rate is zero, the reward guard is
disabled, and the same kernel receives the same observed state. Its scores and selected action agree.
Both procedures reveal the same next potential reward from that arm, so the kernel states still
agree. Repeat inductively. The evidence updates do not affect decisions in this mode and consume

no action random numbers. Any auxiliary kernel state, including its clock and random-number

between a completed observation and the next action and resets no count, interval, phase, or ledger.

S4.6    Implementation arithmetic and branch identity
The mathematical contract uses exact interval inversion, phase arithmetic, and budget comparisons.
The frozen implementation uses 35 bisection steps with outward mixture endpoints and a 10−12
budget-comparison tolerance. Planning regularization was described in Supplement S2. At a
cumulative phase close to an integer, floating addition can delay an attempt by one round. These
choices belong to the numerical implementation; the displayed exact phase identity is not asserted
as a bitwise identity for every floating trajectory.
    The production batch rule has two ledgers. If a proposed service fails its certification check, it
draws the chosen reward kernel’s scores once and maximizes them over the reward-feasible set. It
does not necessarily choose a largest-MC-index arm. No unexecuted proposal reveals a reward. Each
completed choice is followed by exactly one physical observation and one update to the selected
arm’s actual count and sum. The earlier single-ledger development branch is not this production
rule. The interactive interface implements the same dual-budget decision contract; its corrected
ALL-controller wrapper changes only that comparator’s observed proposal-cost input and does not
alter the F-CCP branch.

S16.1    Distinct studies and prespecified controls
The first CCP study and the subsequent frontier/Shared study are development predecessors with
their own frozen protocols. Their tests contain 13,440 and 12,672 records, respectively. They
preserve unsuccessful candidates and negative comparisons; they are not added to the F-CCP win
counts. The earlier checkpoint comparison is retained in Supplementary Section S17. Its 5,760
observations, four environments and resource measurements remain separate.
    Within the F-CCP study, the three valid development rounds contain 2,640, 5,616 and 5,760
trajectories (14,016 in total). The initial development compilation failure generated no valid
trajectory; its protocol and failure logs remain in the source archive. The final temperature and
reward guard were selected using the recorded development rule, before the test. Development and
test seeds and numerical environments differ, although their task types overlap. These archives
document selection rather than provide independent repetitions of the main test.
    Cold-TS-unprotected removes the reward guard while retaining temperature .5; Warm-TS-guard
changes the temperature schedule. The fixed-rate variant, the uncapped certification variant and
the temperature-one variant each preserve the settings stated in the frozen method map. Their full
scenario results include unfavorable comparisons. These are configuration comparisons, not evidence
that an untested mechanism caused the observed gain. No configuration was retuned during this
manuscript reanalysis.

S16.2    Comparator correction and numerical qualifications
The original adaptive ALL planner returned the proposed arm together with zero as its estimated
sampling cost. That second return value is irrelevant when the service rate is fixed or equals one, but
changes the interior adaptive controller. The corrected planner returns the gap between the largest
projected mean and the proposed arm’s projected mean, exactly as in F-CCP. The comparator
correction was separately frozen after the audit and rerun on the same 12 scenarios and 96 seeds. No
F-CCP or reward-baseline parameter changed. Both versions remain in the archive; the corrected
version replaces the original in primary comparisons and is not averaged with it.
    The mathematical phase rule uses exact arithmetic. The frozen implementation has 1,035 runs
in which floating-point accumulation delays a service attempt by one slot at an integer boundary.

S17      Earlier checkpoint comparison
This earlier, separate experiment compares reward allocation and reliable reporting at a fixed physical
budget. They ask how the service rule and the precision of its reporting evidence affect regret
and unresolved area. Its four environments are not the restricted family used for the two-endpoint
separation result, so they do not test its asymptotic rates.

S17.1    Protocol and competing rules
All rewards are Bernoulli. We fix T = 20,000, ε = .30, η = .10, and δ = .05, with eight arms by the
end of each run. There are 160 independent replications per environment. Within a replication,
each method receives the same potential reward stream for each arm: its nth pull of arm i reveals
the same Yi,n . The shared streams do not force the methods to take the same actions. Arrivals
precede the action, and the report follows the observation. All methods retain previous observations.
The configuration records the arrival times, means, seeds, and policy parameters; a policy receives
current arrivals and observed history, without future arrivals, true means, or the evaluation horizon.
    The stable-benchmark environment starts with means .65, .50 and adds three batches of inferior
arms. The successive-improvement environment has benchmark levels .45, .70, .90, .98, so a later
arrival can change an unresolved membership question. The burst environment introduces six arms
at one time, including a new best arm. In the fourth environment arrivals stop at pull 4037, leaving
nearly 16,000 pulls for recovery. Noninitial batches arrive 36 pulls after a 100-pull admission grid;
the short-period baseline is therefore not evaluated only at its most favorable alignment.
    We use two FIFO configurations of Checkpoint-UCB, denoted CP-FIFO in the tables and CP
in the figures, with service every second pull. The finer choice ρ = .04 satisfies 2ρ < η. Once


                                                 S-56
every currently available arm has completed its checkpoint, all η-separated arms are resolved on
the coverage event. This is not the ρ = η/4 = .025 calibration used for the worst-case area bound.
The coarser choice ρ = .12 retains reliable labels but does not guarantee completeness for arbitrary
environments at η = .10. Their eight-arm quota totals are 73,083 and 8,123, respectively. In
particular, the finer configuration cannot complete all quotas within the physical budget.
    Three further rules retain the coarser checkpoint report and change allocation: CP-period8
serves the FIFO queue every eighth pull; CP-confidence uses confidence-driven service every second
pull; and UCB-checkpoint uses full-history UCB without designated service. The confidence-driven
rule samples the widest interval among unresolved arms and the arm with largest upper endpoint,
breaking ties by sample count and arrival order. It stops designated service when every report
is resolved, and a completed checkpoint keeps its fixed interval. These are separately identified
configurations; the FIFO guarantee is not automatically a guarantee for every allocation variant.
    Three methods combine the full-prefix confidence report with a reward or sampling rule. UCB-
prefix admits every arm immediately and uses the same full-history UCB index as Checkpoint-UCB.
