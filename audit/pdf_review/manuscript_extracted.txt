                                                      Abstract
              We study a stochastic multi-armed bandit problem in which new arms arrive exogenously and
          remain available. Performance is measured against the best currently available arm. The resulting
          arrival information discrepancy and drifting benchmark motivate UCB for Arriving Arms (UCB-
          AA), which screens new arms before they enter full competition with incumbents. We analyze
          this two-stage design and give a finite-budget regret analysis based on the comparisons and
          sample prefixes actually used. The analysis separates learning among admitted arms from delayed
          admission and loss of an earlier benchmark. We then ask what statistical benefit can accompany
          screening beyond a smaller internal active set. For simultaneous, reliable reporting of the relative
          near-optimal set, uncertainty about one arrival can obstruct many earlier certifications. We
          characterize a worst-schedule maintenance cost and identify the polynomial arrival boundary for
          sublinear unresolved time. A repeated-update family shows that prompt resolution of temporary
          membership questions can require inverse-square regret, whereas allowing more waiting admits
          a logarithmic upper bound. The necessary cost also holds with fixed positive probability. A
          separate checkpoint construction supplies attainable reporting bounds. Numerical evidence
          distinguishes the archived UCB-AA implementation from this construction and exposes the
          finite-budget cost of conservative certification.

Keywords: sequential experimentation; adaptive screening; arriving arms; dynamic regret; arrival
information discrepancy; drifting benchmark.

    Motivated by such problems, we study the multi-armed bandit problem with arriving arms
(MAB-AA). Let At denote the set of arms available at time t, with At ⊆ At+1 . New arms arrive
over time and remain available thereafter. Performance is evaluated against the best arm currently
available, since a terminal best arm need not have been available earlier.
    Relative to the classical stochastic bandit setting, MAB-AA introduces two main difficulties.
The first is arrival information discrepancy (AID): newly arrived arms must be explored, which
repeatedly diverts samples away from well-understood incumbent arms. The second is a drifting
benchmark (DB): as better arms enter the system, both the identity of the optimal arm and the
regret benchmark may change over time.
    The setting retains stationary, unstructured reward laws and an external arrival process. Arms
are not drawn on demand from a reservoir (Berry et al., 1997), and do not disappear as in mortal
bandits (Chakrabarti et al., 2008). Permanent arrivals are a monotone special case of sleeping-bandit
availability (Kleinberg et al., 2010). Benchmark changes are caused by new arms, rather than
changes in the reward laws of existing arms as in nonstationary bandits (Besbes et al., 2014; Auer
et al., 2019). Ballooning bandits already use permanent arrivals and regret against the best currently
available arm (Ghalme et al., 2021); we do not claim either as a new model or comparator.
    From a statistical perspective, multi-armed bandit problems have long been studied as models
for sequential allocation and adaptive experimentation, dating back to Robbins (1952) and the
asymptotically efficient allocation theory of Lai and Robbins (1985); see also Lai (1987) for connec-
tions to adaptive treatment allocation. This viewpoint has motivated a broad statistical literature,
including Bayesian and index-based approaches to sequential allocation (Gittins, 1979; Berry and


                                                  2
Fristedt, 1985), response-adaptive randomization in clinical trials (Zelen, 1969; Wei and Durham,
1978; Villar et al., 2015; Williamson and Villar, 2020; Aziz et al., 2021), covariate-dependent and
nonparametric bandit methods (Woodroofe, 1979; Yang and Zhu, 2002; Rigollet and Zeevi, 2010;
Perchet and Rigollet, 2013), and recent high-dimensional, semiparametric, and transfer-learning
formulations (Qian and Yang, 2016; Qian et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2024). These works share with ours
the broad goal of learning from accumulated evidence to reduce allocation to inferior alternatives,
but do not directly study the permanent exogenous arrival setting considered here.
    Methodologically, the two challenges call for different but complementary design principles. To
address AID, our approach combines elimination steps (Even-Dar et al., 2006; Auer and Ortner, 2010;
Perchet and Rigollet, 2013; Qian and Yang, 2016) with a pre-elimination mechanism tailored to the
expanding action set. The key idea is to prevent the main competition stage from being repeatedly
disrupted by every newly arriving arm. Instead, late arrivals are first screened against incumbent
candidates, so that clearly inferior arms can be removed before receiving the full exploration budget
assigned to serious contenders. To handle DB, the algorithm repeatedly updates the relevant
comparison set as new arms arrive, and the analysis evaluates regret against the best arm currently
available rather than against a fixed hindsight oracle. Based on these ideas, we propose UCB for
Arriving Arms (UCB-AA). For UCB-AA, we derive regret upper bounds that depend explicitly
on the arrival process, establish sublinear dynamic regret when arm-count growth and admission
delay are controlled, study representative lower-bound regimes, and develop an online extension for
unknown horizons.
    UCB for Arriving Arms (UCB-AA) implements this screening design. Its pre-elimination stage
samples an arrival at coarse resolutions before spending the finer budget used for incumbent arms.
Formal elimination then compares the merged active set. A finite-budget analysis must account
for unfinished phases, arrivals waiting for admission, and any loss of a previously admitted best
arm. We give that accounting explicitly and establish a sample-count bound without compressing
historical gaps into a terminal gap.
    The active set alone does not describe what has been learned about all available arms. An
excluded arm may be statistically inferior, or it may never have been admitted. This distinction
leads to a further question within the same MAB-AA setting: what does it cost to maintain reliable
knowledge of the complete relative near-optimal set while collecting reward? The fixed-arm version
is the ALL-ε problem (Mason et al., 2020). Joint reward and identification objectives also have
established precedents (Degenne et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2026; Cho et al., 2025). Our additional
requirement concerns continuing reports under arrivals, with one simultaneous correctness event
and a cumulative cost for unresolved arm time.
    Two effects of the drifting benchmark determine that cost. An uncertain new arm can block
positive certification of many well-studied incumbents. A later improvement can also make a difficult
earlier membership question irrelevant. We prove a common-environment broadcast lower bound
and a worst-schedule maintenance bound, obtaining the polynomial arrival exponent below which
sublinear regret and sublinear unresolved time are jointly possible. In a repeated-update family,
prompt maintenance has matching inverse-square regret bounds at specified horizons, whereas a

larger constant-factor area budget admits a logarithmic regret upper bound. A fixed-probability
lower bound shows that the necessary cost is not confined to rare paths. These are substantive
extensions of the original regret question, rather than guarantees inferred from the size of UCB-AA’s
active set.
    For the reporting upper bounds we use a separate checkpoint construction, CP-UCB-AA. It
preserves accumulated observations but does not remove negatively reported arms from reward selec-
tion. We distinguish its guarantees from those of the two-stage UCB-AA procedure. The numerical
study likewise distinguishes the original archived implementation from the reporting comparisons.
It retains favorable and unfavorable original observations, while showing that conservative fixed
checkpoints can be impractical at moderate budgets.

2.2   Main statistical challenges
Arrival information discrepancy (AID). A first difficulty is arrival information discrepancy.
With a finite sampling budget up to time T , the learner must allocate samples both to incumbent
arms and to newly arriving arms whose rewards are still highly uncertain. Under continual arrivals,
this repeated re-exploration can prevent the learner from concentrating pulls on the best currently
available arm. Consequently, without additional regularity conditions on the arrival process, sublinear
regret may be unattainable.
     Drifting benchmark (DB). In contrast to the classical setting where the best mean reward
is fixed, the benchmark in MAB-AA is time-dependent: r∗ (t) = maxi∈At ri . Since At expands
over time, both the identity of the best arm and the value r∗ (t) may change when new arms
arrive. Consequently, for a fixed arm i, the suboptimal gap r∗ (t) − ri is generally time-varying
(and non-decreasing in t). This drifting benchmark complicates both algorithm design and analysis,
because the learner must continuously compare well-sampled incumbent arms with newly arriving
arms whose estimates are still highly uncertain.

3     UCB for Arriving Arms (UCB-AA)
UCB-AA is an elimination-based procedure organized by rounds. Its main idea is simple: newly
arriving arms are not allowed to compete immediately with well-sampled incumbent arms. Instead,

                                                     5
they are first buffered and screened at lower cost. Only those that survive this screening step
enter full competition in the next stage. This design aims to reduce wasted exploration on clearly
suboptimal late arrivals while preserving the ability to detect genuinely competitive new arms.

3.1   UCB-AA under a physical sampling budget
UCB for Arriving Arms (UCB-AA) uses two stages of screening. At the beginning of round m,
admit the buffered batch Am and form Bm = Cm−1 ∪ Am , where Cm−1 contains the surviving
incumbents. For m ≥ 2, screen the new arms in phases p = 0, . . . , m − 2 against the incumbents
and the remaining new arms; only new arms can be removed. Then apply formal elimination
to the merged set in phases m − 1, m. Round zero uses phase zero, and round one uses formal
phases zero and one. Empty preliminary batches are no-ops. This retains the distinction between
pre-elimination, which addresses arrival information discrepancy, and formal elimination after new
arms have entered the main competition.

including arrivals at sm : Am = Asm \ A(m − 1), where A(m − 1) = j<m Aj . Arrivals during a call

4     Regret lower bounds in representative arrival regimes
We consider two representative lower-bound regimes for regret. The first describes the evidence
needed to reject inferior arrivals when the best arm is already available. Its static special case is a
classical calibration (Lai and Robbins, 1985; Burnetas and Katehakis, 1996). The second describes
successive local comparisons as the benchmark improves. Both concern regret at a deterministic
physical horizon. They are separate from the reporting requirements introduced below, and are not
claimed as matching converses to an upper bound evaluated at a random round-completion time.

5     Reliable maintenance of the near-optimal set

available arms, and dynamic-regret comparator remain those of Section 2. Reporting a relative
near-optimal set is an additional statistical task; it is not identified with UCB-AA’s active or
surviving set. We use separate report notation to keep that distinction visible.

9    Discussion
We studied stochastic multi-armed bandits with arriving arms, where the available action set
expands and performance is measured against the best currently available arm. Arrival information
discrepancy and a drifting benchmark create repeated learning costs even though every arm has
a stationary reward law. The two-stage UCB-AA design addresses the information imbalance by
screening arrivals before full competition with incumbents. The revised regret account separates
learning, admission delay, and loss of an earlier admitted benchmark. Its finite-budget guarantee is
conditional on the stated calibration and retains the admission-delay term explicitly.
     The additional maintenance analysis makes precise a statistical question that the original active-
set comparison left open. A smaller internal set does not establish reliable knowledge of the complete
near-optimal set. One uncertain arrival can obstruct many earlier certifications, and a drifting
benchmark can make difficult unresolved questions expire. The broadcast and repeated-update
results characterize costs arising from these two effects. Their assumptions, including the restricted
update family and the different allowed area budgets, are part of the conclusions. The worst area
lower bound already occurs in a static initial batch; the temporary-task results supply a separate
dynamic mechanism.
     UCB-AA and CP-UCB-AA serve different purposes in this analysis. The former retains the
original screening architecture. The latter is a constructive reporting rule whose service precision
is fixed in advance and whose unconditional reward analysis does not require correct reports. Its
conservative checkpoints may be ineffective at moderate budgets, and its numerical cost is not
uniformly competitive. Neither a negative report nor a smaller reported set is treated as a reduction
in total storage.
     The supplement supplies full proofs of the quantile lower bound in Section 7 and the compatible-
window truncation result. It also gives eventual exact classification by a separate revisable mul-
tiresolution construction when arrivals stop, the final arm count is finite, and no arm lies on the


                                                  25
membership boundary. That conclusion does not apply to fixed-precision checkpoints.
    The general frontier under overlapping arrivals, automatic precision selection, and a matching
converse for deferred maintenance remain open. The archived UCB-AA study also leaves a concrete
empirical obligation: the repaired procedure must be compared under the same admission and
retained-history rules before attributing a numerical gain to pre-elimination. Contextual information,
delayed feedback, and post-arrival reward drift remain natural extensions of the original MAB-AA
formulation.


Data and code availability
No original empirical or human-subject outcome data were collected. The original simulation
configurations, endpoint records, source snapshots, and figures are preserved separately from the
new reporting experiments. The release contains the 5,760 reporting runs, their fixed configuration,
code, and checks. The oncology illustration is the historical motivation from the original submission,
based on public approval records.
