                                                     Abstract

              We study the cost of maintaining reliable reports of all relatively near-optimal arms while
          collecting rewards when new arms arrive and remain available. Reward laws are stationary, but
          each arrival can change the membership boundary. We measure reporting progress by cumulative
          unresolved arm time outside a prescribed boundary band, under simultaneous correctness of
          all issued labels. Uncertainty about one new arm can obstruct positive certification of many
          earlier candidates. A lower bound in one common reference environment quantifies this effect.
          We obtain matching worst-schedule orders for unresolved area and an arrival-growth boundary
          for jointly sublinear area and expected regret. On a specified repeated-update family, rapid
          maintenance requires inverse-square regret, whereas a constant-factor larger area budget admits
          a logarithmic regret upper bound. The rapid cost also holds with nonvanishing probability in a
          fixed environment. A fixed-precision checkpoint construction provides the upper bounds without
          knowing future arrivals. Finite-budget comparisons show that its conservative certification cost
          can be substantial; they do not establish uniform numerical superiority or a general optimal
          regret–area frontier.

Keywords: sequential experimentation; arriving arms; near-optimal set maintenance; dynamic regret;
simultaneous inference.


1        Introduction
Sequential experiments often acquire new candidates while earlier ones remain available. An
allocation rule must learn which candidates yield high rewards. An experimenter may also need to
know which of all available candidates are within a specified tolerance of the current best. These
objectives need not favor the same observations. A candidate can be sufficiently inferior to receive

     We study these objectives together under permanent exogenous arrivals and stationary reward
laws. If r∗ (t) is the largest mean among the arms available at time t, an arm is relatively near-optimal
when its mean is at least r∗ (t) − ε. Reports certify membership, certify exclusion, or leave the arm
unresolved. We require one simultaneous correctness event for all labels at all times. To rule out
indefinite abstention, we charge the cumulative unresolved time of arms whose means lie more than
η from the membership boundary. The question is how much regret is necessary, and how much is
attainable, under this reporting requirement.
     New arrivals affect the task in two ways. An uncertain new candidate may be better than
the incumbent by enough to invalidate many existing positive reports. Evidence about that one
candidate is then relevant to many reports at once. A later improvement can also make an earlier
difficult membership question unnecessary: a candidate close to the old boundary may be far below
the new one. Its existing upper confidence bound can be combined with a new reference’s lower
bound to certify exclusion. Neither effect requires the reward law of an old arm to change.
     Our observation model and regret comparator have established precedents. Permanent arrivals
form a monotone special case of sleeping-bandit availability (Kleinberg et al., 2010); ballooning
bandits already compare reward with the best arm currently available (Ghalme et al., 2021).
Exogenous arrivals differ from drawing arms on demand from a reservoir (Berry et al., 1997) and
from the disappearing actions of mortal bandits (Chakrabarti et al., 2008). Benchmark improvements
here arise from new arms, whereas nonstationary bandits allow the reward distributions of existing
arms to change (Besbes et al., 2014; Auer et al., 2019). Sequential allocation and adaptive
experimentation have a long statistical history (Robbins, 1952; Lai and Robbins, 1985; Lai, 1987);
the expanding set of actions retains that allocation problem and adds an evolving inferential target.
     For a fixed arm set, identifying every relatively near-optimal arm is the ALL-ε problem (Mason
et al., 2020). Joint reward and identification objectives also have established precedents (Degenne
et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2026; Cho et al., 2025). Our task requires continuing reports under
arrivals, with all-time label reliability and an explicit cumulative cost for unresolved arm time. The
distinction concerns the reporting protocol and its cost, rather than a new arriving-arm model. The
simultaneous recovery scales used in our lower bounds build on existing information inequalities
and multiple-recovery arguments (Kaufmann et al., 2016; Malloy and Nowak, 2014; Jamieson and
Jain, 2018).
     We give three results about maintenance cost. First, a common-reference lower bound shows how
one uncertain arrival can obstruct many other certifications. Its information queue accumulates cost
over time within one reward environment. Second, we obtain matching worst-schedule orders for
cumulative unresolved area, including finite-horizon truncation, and derive the polynomial arrival
boundary for jointly sublinear area and expected regret. This worst-case lower bound already
occurs in an initial static batch; the common-reference result describes a separate consequence of
continuing arrivals. Third, on a specified repeated-update family, rapid resolution of temporary
membership tasks has matching inverse-square regret costs. Allowing a constant-factor larger area

budget admits a logarithmic regret upper bound. A nonvanishing-probability lower bound holds in
one fixed environment under the same cumulative-area requirement. The deferred endpoint has
only an upper bound, and the general frontier over intermediate budgets and overlapping arrivals

    An explicit fixed-precision procedure, Checkpoint-UCB, supplies the attainability results. It
shares all observations between certification and reward learning and retains every arrived arm
in reward selection. The construction separates the statistical costs cleanly, but its conservative
quotas can exceed a practical budget. Our numerical study retains those failures as well as favorable
comparisons. Section 2 defines the joint task, Section 3 gives the construction, and Sections 4–5
establish the maintenance and regret bounds. Section 6 examines their finite-budget implications.

2.3   How arrivals change the reporting problem
For a fixed old arm, ∆i (t) is nondecreasing, so its true membership can change only from member
to nonmember. Its boundary distance |∆i (t) − ε| need not be monotone. A new candidate can first
create uncertainty about the current best mean, obstructing positive certification of many old arms
even when those arms remain truly near-optimal. A sufficiently good new reference can later move
the boundary past earlier candidates, making their fine classification unnecessary. Sections 4 and 5
quantify these two effects under the same reporting protocol.

6     Numerical studies
The experiments compare reward allocation and reliable reporting at a fixed physical budget. They
ask how the service rule and the precision of its reporting evidence affect regret and unresolved area.
The four environments are not the restricted family used for the two-endpoint separation result, so
they do not test its asymptotic rates.




                                                   13
6.1   Protocol and competing rules
All rewards are Bernoulli. We fix T = 20,000, ε = .30, η = .10, and δ = .05, with eight arms by the
end of each run. There are 160 independent replications per environment. Within a replication,
each method receives the same potential reward stream for each arm: its nth pull of arm i reveals
the same Yi,n . The shared streams do not force the methods to take the same actions. Arrivals
precede the action, and the report follows the observation. All methods retain previous observations.
The configuration records the arrival times, means, seeds, and policy parameters; a policy receives
current arrivals and observed history, without future arrivals, true means, or the evaluation horizon.
    The stable-benchmark environment starts with means .65, .50 and adds three batches of inferior
arms. The successive-improvement environment has benchmark levels .45, .70, .90, .98, so a later
arrival can change an unresolved membership question. The burst environment introduces six arms
at one time, including a new best arm. In the fourth environment arrivals stop at pull 4037, leaving
nearly 16,000 pulls for recovery. Noninitial batches arrive 36 pulls after a 100-pull admission grid;
the short-period baseline is therefore not evaluated only at its most favorable alignment.
    We use two FIFO configurations of Checkpoint-UCB, denoted CP-FIFO in the tables and CP
in the figures, with service every second pull. The finer choice ρ = .04 satisfies 2ρ < η. Once
every currently available arm has completed its checkpoint, all η-separated arms are resolved on
the coverage event. This is not the ρ = η/4 = .025 calibration used for the worst-case area bound.
The coarser choice ρ = .12 retains reliable labels but does not guarantee completeness for arbitrary
environments at η = .10. Their eight-arm quota totals are 73,083 and 8,123, respectively. In
particular, the finer configuration cannot complete all quotas within the physical budget.
    Three further rules retain the coarser checkpoint report and change allocation: CP-period8
serves the FIFO queue every eighth pull; CP-confidence uses confidence-driven service every second
pull; and UCB-checkpoint uses full-history UCB without designated service. The confidence-driven
rule samples the widest interval among unresolved arms and the arm with largest upper endpoint,
breaking ties by sample count and arrival order. It stops designated service when every report
is resolved, and a completed checkpoint keeps its fixed interval. These are separately identified
configurations; the FIFO guarantee is not automatically a guarantee for every allocation variant.
    Three methods combine the full-prefix confidence report with a reward or sampling rule. UCB-
prefix admits every arm immediately and uses the same full-history UCB index as Checkpoint-UCB.

7    Discussion
Reliable maintenance has costs that reward allocation alone does not measure. An uncertain arrival
can obstruct many positive reports, while a later improvement can end an earlier fine classification
task. The common-reference lower bound and the repeated-update construction isolate these effects.
The matching worst-schedule area bound answers a different question: its lower bound is already
attained by a static initial batch. The corresponding growth consequences depend on whether the
guarantee concerns one infinite schedule or a uniform terminal-count class.
    Checkpoint-UCB is an explicit attainability construction with a predetermined reporting precision.
It reuses accumulated evidence and keeps all arrived arms eligible for rewards. Its upper bounds
describe achievable statistical costs, but do not imply competitive constants at moderate budgets.
In the numerical study the finer checkpoints never produced a determinate label, and the coarser
configuration was worse than the retained-history identification adaptation in both displayed costs
in two environments. Practical comparisons must therefore match the reporting task and required
service level, as well as reward cost.
    On the specified repeated-update family, rapid maintenance has matching expected-regret and
joint-quantile orders. The deferred endpoint supplies an upper bound at a larger area budget. A
matching deferred converse, the frontier at intermediate budgets, and achievable guarantees under
general overlapping arrivals remain unresolved. Automatic precision selection also needs a valid
confidence construction; choosing a favorable checkpoint after inspecting its observations is outside
the fixed-precision guarantee. Mechanism experiments that separate evidence reuse from service
intensity and boundary-band work would help explain finite-budget performance.
    The supplement gives compatible-window truncations and a general information-allocation
relaxation as necessary conditions, together with intermediate service configurations. It also proves
eventual exact classification for a separate revisable, multiresolution procedure when arrivals stop,
the final arm set is finite, and no arm lies on the membership boundary. That consistency statement
has different assumptions and does not apply to fixed checkpoints. Contextual information, delayed
observations, and changing reward laws would require further analysis.
