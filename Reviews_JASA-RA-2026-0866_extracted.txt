  Review Report for “Multi-Armed Bandits with Arriving Arms:
 Sequential Screening, Dynamic Regret, and Sublinear Guarantees”


Summary
This paper studies a stochastic multi-armed bandit problem in which new arms arrive over time and
remain available, with regret measured relative to the best arm currently available. The problem
is related to the ballooning multi-armed bandit setting studied by Ghalme et al. (2021), but the
proposed UCB-AA algorithm takes a different approach. Specifically, it adopts a round-based
elimination procedure in which newly admitted arms first undergo preliminary screening against
incumbent arms, and the surviving arms then enter a formal elimination stage together with the
incumbents. The authors provide regret bounds, an online extension, lower bounds for representative
regimes, and simulation studies comparing regret, wasted pulls, and the size of the active set.
The idea of screening newly arrived arms before they enter full competition is intuitive, and the
paper develops its theoretical analysis in a logically structured manner. However, I have several
concerns about the regret decomposition, the relationship between the round schedule and the
actual pull budget, and several steps in the proofs. At the same time, some details of the simulation
settings and implementation are not sufficiently clear, which makes the numerical results difficult to
reproduce and interpret. These issues may affect both the justification of the main regret guarantees
and the assessment of the practical benefits of the proposed method.
The comments are based on my reading of the manuscript and my own implementation of the
algorithm. Page and line references below refer to the page numbers at the top of each page and
the line numbers in the left margin.

  (b) Delays in admitting new arms. Arms arriving during round m wait until round m + 1.
      An arm arriving near the beginning of a long round therefore waits for almost its entire
      length. Proposition 1 bounds this delay under Assumption 2, but it remains unclear why
      admission should follow the increasingly long elimination rounds, rather than a shorter
      interval. For example, one could modify the paper’s Growing UCB1 baseline to admit waiting
      arms every fixed H pulls and eliminate arm i when UCBi (t) < maxj∈Ct LCBj (t), using each
      arm’s accumulated observations, where Ct is the set of admitted arms that remain active. A
      comparison with shorter admission intervals would help establish whether waiting until the
      end of a round reduces subsequent exploration enough to justify the additional delay.

4. The simulation settings and the practical benefits of the method need to be clarified.
  (a) Comparison with Growing MOSS. The simulations do not show a clear overall advantage over
      Growing MOSS. UCB-AA has higher regret and more wasted pulls in Scenario 1, whereas in
      Scenario 2 it has similar final regret and fewer wasted pulls. The regret curves in Scenario 2
      suggest that UCB-AA may compare more favorably over a longer horizon, which would be
      useful to examine. The authors also emphasize the benefit of maintaining a smaller active
      set, but its practical value needs further experimental support.
  (b) Differences between the main text and supplement. The main text and supplement give
      different settings: r0 = 0.6 versus 0.7 in Scenario 1 (pp. 25 and 92), initial near-optimal
      means in [0.7, 0.8] versus [0.89, 0.90] in Scenario 2 (pp. 26 and 91–92), and T versus t in the
      Growing MOSS index (p. 26 and Algorithm 5, p. 94). These differences change the generated
      data or the comparison method. The specifications used for the figures need to be identified
      and made consistent.
  (c) The choice of M in the simulations. The value of M used in the simulations is not reported.
      In my attempt to reproduce the experiments from the description, I did not fully recover
      the reported results. The authors should specify the value of M and report the number and
      lengths of rounds completed within 60000 pulls.

   • The simulations introduce µ and related mean parameters without connecting them to ri

   • On p. 8, Lines 23–25, the description introduces “phases p = 0, . . . , m − 2” and “the last two
     phases p = m − 1, m” before explaining what one phase consists of.
   • The text refers to completing M rounds, whereas Algorithm 1 runs rounds 0, . . . , M , giving
     M + 1 rounds.
   • rˆ︁i (np ) in Algorithm 1 (p. 9) is not explicitly defined when first used, and the proof provides
     no formal definition either. R(i) (π) on p. 40 is not standard notation and should be explicitly
     defined by a formula.
2. The figure presentation could be improved in terms of aesthetics and formatting. For example,
   labels such as “eps=0.1” could be formatted using mathematical notation, such as ϵ = 0.1.
